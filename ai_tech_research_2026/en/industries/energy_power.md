## 0. The Industry in One Sentence

The energy industry takes the energy found in nature (oil, natural gas, coal, uranium, sunlight, wind, geothermal heat), turns it into forms people can use, and delivers it to **the right place** at **the right time**.

The hardest part is electricity. Electricity can hardly be stored in bulk, so **generation and consumption must stay in balance every single second**, or the power grid (电网) collapses. Once you understand this, you can understand most of the rules, prices and conflicts in this industry.

## 2. How the Money Flows

### 2.1 Three Ways to Make Money in Electricity

**Model 1: Regulated grid companies earn a return based on how much they invest**

Most US utilities and China's grid companies are "natural monopolies (自然垄断)": a region needs only one grid, so the government lets one company hold a monopoly (垄断) but regulates its prices.

In the US, state regulators approve a rate of return that a utility may earn on its investment (the allowed return on equity (ROE, 股本回报率) is generally around 9 to 10 percent). This creates an interesting incentive: **the more a utility invests, the more money it can make**, so utilities like to build new lines and substations, and those costs end up in household electricity bills.

This is one of the core tensions in the US today:
* US residential electricity prices in 2026 are about 18.3 cents per kilowatt hour (kWh, 千瓦时), about 5 percent higher than a year earlier and about 37 percent higher than in 2020
* Utilities filed a record number of rate cases (涨价申请) in 2025
* S&P's assessment is that "affordability (可负担性)" has gone from a background issue to a hard constraint on how much utilities can earn

**Model 2: Market based generators sell electricity in the wholesale market (批发电力市场)**

In many parts of the US, power plants bid to sell electricity in a wholesale market. Besides selling energy, there is another very important source of revenue called the "**capacity payment**": the grid operator holds auctions several years ahead and pays power plants to guarantee that they can generate when needed.

The capacity auction (容量拍卖) run by PJM, the largest grid region in the US (covering Washington, Philadelphia, Chicago and more across 13 states), is a window into how this industry works:
* The December 2025 auction for 2027 to 2028 cleared right at the price cap (333 dollars per megawatt (MW, 兆瓦) per day), for a record total cost of 16.4 billion dollars. It was the third record high price in a row and the second auction in a row to hit the cap
* Even so, it still came up about 6.6 gigawatts (GW, 吉瓦) short of the reliability requirement
* The market monitor estimates that about 6.3 to 6.5 billion dollars of that cost was driven by data centers
* One NRDC projection is that if this trend continues, a typical household's monthly bill could rise by about 70 dollars by 2028
* The 2028 to 2029 auction, announced on July 14, 2026, hit the cap again (the cap was lowered slightly to 325 dollars per MW per day), making it the third auction in a row at the cap. The shortfall widened to about 6.8 GW. PJM estimates that without the cap the price would have been about 555 dollars per MW per day

**Model 3: Selling equipment and engineering**

Right now it is a seller's market. The clearest examples are gas turbines and transformers:
* GE Vernova's gas turbine backlog (积压订单), including reserved production slots, reached 116 GW in the second quarter of 2026 and is expected to reach at least 125 GW by the end of the year. Yet it can build only about 20 GW a year, and deliveries of heavy duty gas turbines are essentially booked through 2028
* Lead times (交期) for large transformers run as long as 4 years; generator step up transformers used at power plants take more than 160 weeks

**China**: China's grid used to run on "unified purchase and unified sale (统购统销)," with electricity prices set by the government. It is now moving toward markets: since June 1, 2025, new wind and solar projects have shifted from fixed tariffs to market pricing; by September 2026, 11 provincial electricity spot markets (现货市场) were formally operating; the goal is to basically complete a unified national electricity market by 2030.

### 2.2 Oil and Gas: Price Decides Everything

Profits at oil and gas companies depend mainly on oil prices, and oil prices depend mainly on geopolitics and OPEC+. 2026 is an extreme example:
* The Iran war broke out in late February and the Strait of Hormuz (霍尔木兹海峡) closed; Brent crude rose from about 72 dollars before the war to nearly 120 dollars in March, and briefly touched about 126 dollars on April 30, a four year high
* In late June, after the US and Iran agreed to stop attacking each other, it fell back to the low 70s, close to its prewar level; around August 26, on hopes that the strait would reopen, it was about 86 dollars
* In September the conflict escalated again and Brent climbed back above 100 dollars; on October 12 it was still around 103 dollars. Oil prices can swing wildly within a few months: in September, forecasts from various institutions for fourth quarter prices ranged from 77 to 105 dollars, a very wide spread

Natural gas took an even bigger hit. About 20 percent of the world's LNG passes through the Strait of Hormuz. Qatar's main LNG facilities were knocked offline by drone attacks in March, and Asian LNG prices jumped sharply within days.

### 2.3 Where the Profits Are Now

* **Making money**: equipment makers sitting on large backlogs (GE Vernova), generators that own scarce sources of power (especially nuclear), and grid companies investing at record levels. State Grid plans to invest 4 trillion yuan from 2026 to 2030, about 40 percent more than in the previous five years; US utilities plan to invest about 1.1 trillion dollars from 2025 to 2029
* **Not making money**: Chinese solar manufacturers. Overcapacity (产能过剩) is severe, the leading companies have lost money for about 10 straight quarters, and polysilicon (多晶硅) inventory exceeds 540,000 tonnes. This is a textbook case of "perfect competition (完全竞争)": the product keeps getting cheaper and more widespread, but the companies that make it cannot make money

## 4. Major Upheavals in History

| When | What happened | Winners | Losers |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1973 and 1979 | Two oil crises; OPEC gains pricing power | Oil producing countries; France seized the moment to build nuclear power on a large scale and still gets about 70 percent of its electricity from nuclear | Oil importing countries |
| 1990s | Many countries split generation from the grid and set up wholesale electricity markets | Efficient generators | The California electricity crisis of 2000 to 2001 and Enron's bankruptcy in 2001 showed how costly a flawed market design can be |
| 2008 to 2015 | The US shale revolution (页岩革命): horizontal drilling plus hydraulic fracturing (压裂) made the US the world's largest oil and gas producer | US consumers, as natural gas became cheap | Coal power; many shale companies themselves went bankrupt when oil prices fell, producing huge volumes but earning poor returns |
| 2010s | The cost of solar and batteries collapsed, with China dominating manufacturing | Electricity users; China took the lead in output across the board | Solar manufacturing in Europe and the US; Chinese manufacturers themselves fell into a price war (价格战) |
| 2011 | The Fukushima nuclear accident | Natural gas and renewables | Nuclear power; Germany decided to phase out nuclear entirely |
| 2022 | The Russia Ukraine war and Europe's natural gas crisis | LNG exporters, especially the US and Qatar | European industry that depended on Russian gas |
| 2024 to 2026 | AI electricity demand surges, plus the 2026 Iran war | Equipment makers, nuclear power, grid companies | Households facing rising electricity bills; importers dependent on the Strait of Hormuz |

**Three lessons from history**:
1. **Cheaper technology spreads a product, but it does not necessarily make the manufacturers rich.** Shale oil and solar both show this. In Thiel's terms, they created enormous value but failed to capture it
2. **Every energy crisis reshapes policy.** Strategic reserves (战略储备), nuclear power and LNG infrastructure were all built in the wake of crises
3. **Market rules decide who makes money.** Enron and the California crisis, and PJM's capacity auctions, all show that designing the rules is itself a big business

## 5. What Is Changing Now

### 5.1 Demand Is Growing Again

For more than a decade, electricity consumption in developed countries was essentially flat. That has changed:
* Global electricity demand grew 3 percent in 2025, and the IEA expects it to grow 3.6 percent a year from 2026 to 2030
* The Energy Information Administration (EIA) expects US electricity use to set a new record of 4,288 billion kilowatt hours in 2026; the IEA estimates that about half of US demand growth through 2030 will come from data centers
* Global data center electricity use is expected to double from 485 terawatt hours (TWh, 太瓦时) in 2025 to 950 TWh by 2030; electricity use by AI data centers grew 50 percent in 2025
* Beyond data centers, electric vehicles, air conditioning and industrial electrification are also pushing demand up

### 5.2 No Power Available: The Interconnection Queue (并网排队)

Before a new power plant can connect to the grid, a step called interconnection (并网), it has to wait in line while the grid company studies the project, approves it and upgrades the lines. Data from Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory:
* By the end of 2025, about **2,061 GW** of generation and storage projects were in the queue, more than the entire installed capacity of the US
* For projects completed in 2025, the median time from application to commercial operation was **more than 5 years**
* Measured by capacity, only **13 percent** of the projects that applied to connect from 2000 to 2020 were eventually built, and 75 percent withdrew
* Among new applications in 2025, natural gas projects rose 86 percent, while solar, wind and storage all declined

### 5.3 Not Enough Equipment, Not Enough People

* Gas turbine deliveries are essentially booked through 2028, and large transformers can take up to 4 years to arrive
* The US needs about 81,000 new electricians a year; 59 percent of utilities say they cannot hire enough line workers
* Electrical work accounts for 45 to 70 percent of the cost of building a data center; Microsoft's president has said the shortage of electricians is the number one obstacle to data center expansion in the US

### 5.4 US Policy Is Shifting Sharply

* **Tax credit (税收抵免) cuts**: the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (大而美法案), passed in July 2025, requires wind and solar projects that start construction after July 4, 2026 to be in service by the end of 2027, or they lose federal tax credits
* **Inverter ban**: on July 28 the FCC added foreign produced inverters to its "Covered List (涵盖清单)," so new models can no longer be authorized. A revision on August 20 narrowed the scope to grid tied inverters capable of remote connectivity, and products eligible for the 45X tax credit no longer count as foreign produced. To count as domestic, US components must exceed 65 percent of component cost (domestic content, 本土含量) through 2028 and 75 percent from 2029; see [personal_fit.md](../personal_fit.md) for details
* **Connecting large loads (负荷)**: the Department of Energy asked FERC to write rules for connecting data centers and other large loads to the grid. In June FERC ordered six regional grid operators to revise or justify their current rules, but formal rules have not yet been issued

### 5.5 China: From a Race to Install to a Race to Build Markets

* China added a record of about 315 GW of solar in 2025; but only 72 GW in the first half of 2026, down 66 percent year over year (同比), because new projects moved to market pricing and returns are no longer certain; from January to August it added about 97 GW, down about 58 percent, so the decline is starting to narrow
* The government is pushing "anti involution (反内卷)," hoping to cut output and raise prices. In late September the average polysilicon transaction price recovered to more than 40,000 yuan per tonne, about 10,000 yuan higher than at the end of July, but mainly on expectations of output cuts; trading is thin and inventory is still above 540,000 tonnes
* State Grid's investment is at a record, with plans to build 15 new ultra high voltage (UHV, 特高压) lines from 2026 to 2030
* Electricity spot markets are rolling out province by province

### 5.6 New Technologies Start to Deliver

| Technology | Progress in 2026 |
| :--- | :--- |
| Energy storage | The average battery pack (电池包) price fell to 108 dollars per kilowatt hour in 2025; batteries for stationary storage fell 45 percent to 70 dollars, becoming the cheapest segment for the first time |
| Virtual power plants | About 38 to 40 GW are registered in the US; the Department of Energy's target is 80 to 160 GW by 2030; on September 9, 140,000 home batteries in California together sent 580 MW into the grid in a single evening |
| Enhanced geothermal | Fervo's Cape Station in Utah first generated power on September 24 (one 33 MW unit first, out of a first phase of about 100 MW), which Fervo calls the world's first commercial scale enhanced geothermal project to reach first power; in early September it signed a 396 MW power purchase agreement (PPA, 购电协议) with Google, with deliveries starting in 2028; Fervo listed on Nasdaq in May |
| Nuclear fusion | At Commonwealth Fusion Systems in Massachusetts, the SPARC device is about 80 percent complete; first plasma has slipped from 2026 to 2027 |
| Nuclear restarts | The restart of the Palisades nuclear plant in Michigan has been delayed again and again and was still not complete as of October |
| Solid state transformers (固态变压器) | Heron Power raised 140 million dollars in February, DG Matrix raised 60 million dollars, and Amperesand raised 80 million dollars |
| Flexible data centers | In Emerald AI's pilot, a data center cut its power use by 25 percent during peak demand; the company's valuation (估值) is 1.05 billion dollars |

## 6. What People on the Front Line Complain About Most

The following is compiled from industry reporting, not from interviews I conducted myself. If you want to test a direction, the best approach is to ask these people directly.

| Who | Biggest headache |
| :--- | :--- |
| Power plant developers | Interconnection takes more than five years, and they do not know how much they will end up paying for grid upgrades; measured by capacity, three quarters of applications are eventually withdrawn |
| Utilities | Load forecasting has broken down: the same data center project applies to several utilities at once, so it is hard to tell real projects from phantom ones; equipment cannot be bought; workers cannot be hired; regulators are reluctant to approve rate increases |
| Data center developers | "When can we get power?" has become the first criterion for choosing a site; electricians are in short supply |
| Households and small businesses | Electricity bills keep rising, and the bills are hard to understand |
| Chinese solar manufacturers | Overcapacity and losses quarter after quarter |
| Policymakers | Reliable, cheap and low carbon: the three goals are hard to meet at the same time |

## 7. Opportunities

### 7.1 The First Type: Hot Trends (风口), In and Out Fast

| Opportunity | Why now | Signs the wave is receding |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Helping data centers find power: data services and consulting on site selection and "when can we get power" | Time to power has become the first criterion for site selection | Major grid operators publish open, transparent interconnection data, or load growth slows |
| Preparing interconnection paperwork and handling the process on developers' behalf | More than 2,000 GW in the queue, and the process is complex | Interconnection reform simplifies the process |
| Training and recruiting platforms for electricians and grid technicians | Severe shortages, rising wages | The data center building boom cools |
| Electricity bill analysis and demand response enrollment for small and midsize businesses | Electricity prices keep rising | Utilities offer similar tools themselves |
| A marketplace matching buyers and sellers of used and refurbished long lead time equipment | Transformers and similar equipment take years to arrive | Production capacity catches up with demand |
| Selling and installing balcony solar (阳台光伏) and home batteries | States are passing laws one after another, and electricity prices are rising | Price wars, big brands enter |

### 7.2 The Second Type: Directions That Could Grow into Monopolies

**1. Using AI to Speed Up Interconnection Studies**
* **Secret (秘密)**: one big reason the interconnection queue moves so slowly is that grid companies have to run complex power system simulations for every project, such as power flow analysis (潮流计算) and stability analysis, and they do not have enough staff, so each study takes anywhere from months to years. If AI and high performance simulation could cut that to days, and regulators and grid companies accepted the results, the tool would become infrastructure for the whole industry
* **Where the monopoly comes from**: standard status once regulators accept it, plus a growing store of grid model data
* **Does it fit you?**: Very well. It needs power systems knowledge (ECE) plus machine learning
* **What would make it wrong**: grid companies are unwilling to hand critical studies to outside tools; the data cannot be obtained

**2. Flexible Load Scheduling**
* This is Direction 7 in the [two kinds of opportunities](../two_kinds_of_opportunities.md) document. Emerald AI is already doing this in the US, so consider a narrower entry point: small and midsize data centers, edge compute (算力), industrial users

**3. Power Electronics (电力电子): Solid State Transformers and US Made Inverters**
* Solid state transformers replace the traditional iron core and copper coils with semiconductors. They are smaller and easier to control, and they sidestep the supply chain (供应链) bottleneck of traditional transformers. Several companies have already raised large rounds, so look for niches they have not covered, such as charging depots for electric vehicle fleets, campus microgrids and plug in solar (插电式光伏)
* For the US made inverter opportunity, see "New A" in [personal_fit.md](../personal_fit.md)

**4. An "Operating System" for Home Energy**
* **Secret**: home batteries, electric vehicles, heat pumps and balcony solar are turning into many small power plants. Whoever can aggregate and dispatch them will stand between households and the grid, creating two sided network effects (双边网络效应)
* **Competition**: Tesla, Base Power and others are already doing this. You could start with overlooked groups such as renters and multifamily buildings

**5. A Data Layer for Grid Assets**
* Many US transformers and lines have been in service for decades, so there is demand for failure prediction, remaining life assessment and "grid digital twins (电网数字孪生)." Whoever accumulates the most operating data from equipment will make the most accurate predictions

**6. Next Generation Firm Power (稳定电源): Enhanced Geothermal, Nuclear Fusion, Small Modular Reactors**
* These are hard tech bets that take ten years or more and need huge amounts of capital, which makes them very hard for a student startup. A more realistic path is to intern or work at one of these companies. Commonwealth Fusion Systems is in Devens, Massachusetts, not far from you
